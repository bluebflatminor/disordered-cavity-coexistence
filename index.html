Coexistence Conditions for Computation in Driven Disordered Optical Cavities

Why high dimensionality is not enough, and the requirements a substrate would have to meet at the same operating point.

STATUS — PRE-EXPERIMENTAL, WORK IN PROGRESS REVISION 2.3.1 — 2026-10-05 · NEW MATERIAL FROZEN PENDING REF. 5 LICENSE — CC0 / PUBLIC DOMAIN FORM — SHORT NOTE
Work in progress. This note records a developing argument, not a finished result. Revision 2.3 makes corrections from two external reviews that do not depend on unread sources: it defines the system boundary and memory scope, renames the overclaiming “criterion,” separates the kinds of requirement, demotes modal overlap to a diagnostic, and adds a status-of-claims summary. New material remains frozen until reference 5 has been read, because the gap claim in §4 depends on it; the one exception is a counter-example that corrects an overstatement (§4). Sections changed are marked, and unresolved points are collected in §7. No experiment has been run.

A passive optical cavity filled with linear media, driven through its input field, implements a linear input–output map, however elaborate its engineered disorder. Such a map can be of arbitrarily high rank and still compute no nonlinear function of its input history. That statement holds only when the data enter as the drive. When the data instead modulate the cavity's scattering potential, multiple scattering produces a nonlinear map from data to output with purely linear optics.

Whether a driven, open, disordered cavity can be a computational substrate depends on coupled physical observables — a nonlinearity inside the system, dimensionality, memory, readout noise, and reproducibility — that all enter through the same complex spectrum. This note proposes a measurable test for the existence and order of nonlinearity inside the cavity, with the input port and system boundary declared. That test is a necessary condition only. The note then states the further requirements that would have to hold at the same operating point. It shows that these observables are coupled; it does not show that they can all be met, or that they give a complete account of computation.

§ 1

Setup: the open cavity as a non-Hermitian operator

Consider a resonator with a spatially structured permittivity \(\varepsilon(\mathbf r)\) — a plano-hemispherical cavity seeded with subwavelength dielectric scatterers. In the frequency domain the field obeys

(1) \[ \nabla\times\nabla\times\mathbf E(\mathbf r,\omega)-\frac{\omega^{2}}{c^{2}}\,\varepsilon(\mathbf r)\,\mathbf E(\mathbf r,\omega)=i\omega\mu_0\,\mathbf J_{\text{s}}(\mathbf r,\omega), \]

where \(\mathbf J_{\text{s}}\) is the source current at the input ports. With \(\mathbf J_{\text{s}}=0\), Eq. (1) gives the cavity's natural modes; with it, it gives the driven response.

Because the cavity is open — radiative leakage through the curved wall — the relevant operator is non-Hermitian, and its natural modes are quasinormal modes with complex eigenfrequencies

(2) \[ \tilde\omega_{m}=\omega_{m}-i\,\gamma_{m},\qquad \gamma_{m}>0. \]

The real part fixes the spectral position; the imaginary part fixes the linewidth and the photon lifetime. The scatterers break integrability and mix these modes.

Scope and system boundary (new in rev. 2.3)

Boundary. The system runs from the declared input port to the optical field at the output port, before any detector. Modulators, detectors and converters sit outside it. “Inside the cavity” in this note means inside this boundary.

Three different objects. (i) Nonlinear field evolution; (ii) a nonlinear map from data to output field; (iii) nonlinearity that is useful for computation. The kernel test in §4 detects (ii) within the boundary. Under source-port encoding, (ii) implies (i). Under parameter-port encoding, (ii) arises from linear field evolution. Whether either gives (iii) is a task-level question, addressed at the end of §4.

Memory scope. This note studies computation whose memory is carried by the cavity field. Systems whose memory lives in gain, carriers, heat or electronics are outside its scope by definition, not ruled out by argument. That choice is why photon lifetime appears in Eq. (10).

Time invariance. The kernels below assume the map from data to output is time-invariant. Under parameter-port encoding this holds when the optical carrier is steady and only the data vary.

§ 2

The linearity constraint and its scope

revised in rev. 2.1

For linear media, Eq. (1) is linear in \(\mathbf E\). The map from drive to field is therefore a linear operator \(A(\omega)\),

(3) \[ \mathbf E_{\text{out}}(\omega)=A(\omega)\,\mathbf E_{\text{in}}(\omega). \]

Equivalently, expanding the field in quasinormal modes, \(\mathbf E(\mathbf r,t)=\sum_m a_m(t)\,\boldsymbol\psi_m(\mathbf r)\), the modal amplitudes obey a coupled-mode equation

(4) \[ \frac{da_{m}}{dt}=(i\omega_{m}-\gamma_{m})\,a_{m}+\sum_{n}\kappa_{mn}\,a_{n}+N_{m}\!\left[\{a\}\right]+s_{m}(t), \]

where \(\kappa_{mn}\) is the disorder-induced linear mode coupling, \(s_m\) the projected drive, and \(N_m\) the coupling generated by any field-dependence of \(\varepsilon\). In the passive linear limit \(N_m=0\), and the solution is a convolution,

(5) \[ a_{m}(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\! G_{m}(t-t')\,s(t')\,dt'. \]

This is the constraint in one line: when the data enter only through the drive \(s_m(t)\), the internal state is a linear functional of the drive history. No matter how many modes participate or how complex \(A(\omega)\) is, the state never depends on products of its own past. A high-rank linear map can perform useful linear transformations — as a feature projection or a memory substrate — but it cannot realize nonlinear, state-dependent transformations of its input history.

Scope: the constraint is a statement about the input port

Eq. (5) assumes the data reach the cavity only through \(s_m(t)\). If the data instead modulate the scattering potential \(V\propto(\varepsilon-1)\) or the couplings \(\kappa_{mn}\) — a reconfigurable wall, a spatial light modulator acting as a mirror, an intracavity modulator — the field is still linear in \(\mathbf E_{\text{in}}\), but the map from data to output is not. Writing the response as a Born series in \(V\),

(5a) \[ \mathbf E_{\text{out}}=\left[V+V(G_0V)+V(G_0V)^2+\cdots\right]\mathbf E_{\text{in}}, \]

each additional interaction with a data-dependent \(V\) adds one polynomial order in the data.2 This structural nonlinearity has been demonstrated with CW light at low power in multiple-scattering cavities1,2 and in a Fabry–Pérot cavity with one mirror replaced by a spatial light modulator.3 The linearity constraint above therefore applies to source-port encoding only.

§ 3

Observables

revised in rev. 2.3

Dimensionality. Let \(M\) be the matrix of output-port samples (one row per readout channel, one column per input step), with singular values \(\sigma_i\). Singular values below the measured noise floor are discarded first, and the threshold is reported with the result. The effective dimensionality is the participation ratio of what remains,

(6) \[ r_{\text{eff}}=\frac{\left(\sum_i \sigma_i\right)^{2}}{\sum_i \sigma_i^{2}}, \]

In a cavity it reflects the eigenfunction statistics of the mixed mode field, not the detector count.

Memory. The fading-memory timescale is the decay-rate spectrum measured against the drive period: \(\tau_m=1/\gamma_m\), with the cavity scale \(\tau\simeq \mathcal F L/\pi c\) for finesse \(\mathcal F\). Modal individuality is governed by the modal-overlap (Thouless) parameter

(7) \[ \delta=\frac{\bar\gamma}{\Delta\omega}, \]

with \(\Delta\omega\) the mean level spacing. Resolved modes require \(\delta\lesssim1\); for \(\delta\gg1\) resonances overlap. Revision 2.3 treats \(\delta\) as a diagnostic, not a requirement. \(r_{\text{eff}}\) is defined from the readout, not from resolved modes, and strongly overlapping, diffusive regimes can still give high \(r_{\text{eff}}\). No argument yet links resolved modes to computational capacity (§7, item 9).

Nonlinearity. An activation internal to the evolution can enter by two routes.

(a) Field-dependent permittivity. The intensity route,

(8) \[ \varepsilon(\mathbf r)\;\to\;\varepsilon(\mathbf r)+\chi_{\text{eff}}\!\left(\mathbf r,|\mathbf E|^{2}\right), \]

for example saturable gain \(\chi_{\text{eff}}=\chi_0\,(1+I/I_{\text{sat}})^{-1}\) or a Kerr shift \(\Delta n=n_2 I\). What matters is not only its magnitude but its response time relative to \(\tau_m\): a nonlinearity slow compared with the photon lifetime acts as a quasi-static background, not a per-symbol activation.

(b) Data-dependent permittivity. The structural route,

(8a) \[ \varepsilon(\mathbf r)\;\to\;\varepsilon(\mathbf r)+\Delta\varepsilon\!\left(\mathbf r;u(t)\right), \]

needs no field buildup, so it is not limited by intensity. Its cost moves to the modulator, which must switch at a rate comparable to \(1/\tau_{\text{photon}}\) for the resulting nonlinearity to act per symbol rather than as a slowly reconfigured linear map. Formally, the Born series in Eq. (5a) contains every order at once. What photon lifetime changes is how much weight the higher-order terms carry: longer-lived light interacts with the modulated potential more times. In a simulated two-dimensional scattering cavity, the coefficient of determination \(R^2\) of a linear fit falls steadily as wall reflectivity rises.2 The same spectrum \(\{\gamma_m\}\) that sets memory therefore also sets how strongly higher orders contribute. That is a trend in one simulation, not a general law relating lifetime to effective order.

§ 4

The kernel test

revised in rev. 2.3

The nonlinearity question can be stated without reservoir-computing vocabulary, as a statement about the Volterra expansion of the input–output map:

(9) \[ y(t)=\sum_{k\ge1}\;\int\!\cdots\!\int h_{k}(\tau_1,\dots,\tau_k)\prod_{i=1}^{k}u(t-\tau_i)\,d\tau_i. \]

A linear system has only a first-order kernel — \(h_1=G\) and \(h_{k\ge2}\equiv0\). Nonlinearity in the state evolution appears as a nonvanishing higher-order kernel, and the physically meaningful requirement is that it be sourced inside the system boundary of §1 rather than by a nonlinear detector, with temporal support set by the photon lifetime. Let \(k^{*}\) be the lowest order above one whose kernel does not vanish:

(10) \[ \exists\,k\ge2:\;h_{k}\neq0;\qquad k^{*}=\min\{k\ge2: h_k\neq0\},\qquad \operatorname{supp} h_{k^{*}} \sim \tau_{\text{photon}}. \]

This tests one thing: whether, and at what order, nonlinearity arises inside the system boundary. It says nothing about dimensionality, memory capacity, conditioning, reproducibility or task performance, which remain separate observables. In most cases \(k^{*}=2\): drive the system with impulses and impulse pairs and read off whether \(h_2\) survives over the memory window.

Attributing a kernel to the cavity (new in rev. 2.3)

Nonlinearity in the modulator, detector or converter can imitate a cavity kernel. Those instruments act within a single sample, so they put kernel weight near zero delay, on the diagonal \(\tau_1=\tau_2=0\). A kernel generated inside the cavity spreads over delays of order \(\tau_{\text{photon}}\). The proposed controls are: a linear reference path that bypasses the cavity through the same input and output chain; power-scaling tests; and field-linear (coherent) detection where possible. These controls are proposed, not yet tested.

Why the test is not \(h_2\) alone (new in rev. 2.1)

Revision 2 stated the test as \(h_2\neq0\). That excludes genuinely nonlinear systems in which symmetry forbids the second-order term. If the map is odd in the input, so that \(u\to-u\) sends \(y\to-y\), every even-order kernel vanishes. A Kerr (\(\chi^{(3)}\)) cavity with coherent field encoding and phase-sensitive readout is such a case: \(h_2=0\) while \(h_3\neq0\). The test is therefore the lowest nonvanishing kernel of order two or higher. \(h_2\) remains the default probe because it is the cheapest to measure; when an odd symmetry is present, impulse triples are needed to resolve \(h_3\).

What detector nonlinearity can and cannot do (revised in rev. 2.3)

A linear scattering system read out by an intensity detector has only second-order nonlinearity, so it cannot approximate parity functions above order two.2 Detector nonlinearity puts a ceiling on achievable order and adds no state-dependent recurrence. That ceiling is often not binding in practice. A fully passive silicon reservoir, with optical memory from delay lines and its only nonlinearity at the photodetectors, experimentally performed Boolean operations with memory — including XOR of past bits, which linear regression cannot solve — and 5-bit header recognition at up to 12.5 Gbit/s; spoken-digit recognition was shown in simulation only.18 Those tasks are second order, which fits within the ceiling. Nonlinearity inside the cavity therefore matters for tasks that need order above two or state-dependent recurrence, not for computation in general.

The input port must be declared

Eq. (9) is incomplete until it states where \(u\) enters. With source-port encoding, a nonvanishing \(h_{k^{*}}\) certifies route (a). With parameter-port encoding, multiple scattering alone generates one, sourced inside the cavity with no \(\chi^{(3)}\). Either route satisfies (10) only if \(\operatorname{supp}h_{k^{*}}\sim\tau_{\text{photon}}\); for route (b) that requires modulation on the photon-lifetime timescale. Among the structural-nonlinearity demonstrations read for this revision, the maps are static or carry memory electronically: the one time-series forecasting result uses a digital leaky integrator for its reservoir state.4 None reviewed so far shows a structural higher-order kernel with support on the photon-lifetime scale (see §7, item 1).

Existing structural-nonlinearity metrics are complementary to the kernel test: the coefficient of determination of a linear fit and the mean number of interactions \(\langle n\rangle\),3 and the order-resolved Boolean coefficient sums \(S_n\) for binary inputs.2 None of them is time-resolved, which is what (10) adds.

A nonvanishing kernel of order \(k^{*}\) is a necessary condition, not a sufficient one: it shows that nonlinearity exists, not that useful computation does. A substrate can be nonlinear and still compute poorly if its memory capacity or readout conditioning is weak; the sufficient figure of merit is task-level — memory capacity, or accuracy on a benchmark that demands nonlinear memory (NARMA, parity-\(N\)) measured against a linear-readout control. The standard task-independent measure is the information processing capacity, which decomposes a system's capacity by polynomial degree and delay; its degree-two part is, roughly, a task-level counterpart of \(h_2\).16 The same framework reports a general trade-off between the nonlinearity of computation and short-term memory,16 which is one face of the coexistence problem below.

§ 5

The coexistence problem

revised in rev. 2.3

By coexistence we mean the simultaneous satisfaction of the requirements below — not phase or modal coexistence, nor bistability. The difficulty is that the observables are not independent: disorder, loss, gain and the nonlinearity all enter through the same complex spectrum \(\{\omega_m-i\gamma_m\}\) and the same susceptibility.

Disorder against memory. Disorder raises \(r_{\text{eff}}\) but adds radiative loss, which shortens memory and raises the modal overlap \(\delta\). In the structural route the same loss also lowers the nonlinear order, since fewer round trips mean fewer interactions with the modulated potential.2

Nonlinearity against dimensionality. A nonlinearity strong enough to make \(h_2\neq0\) — gain saturation near threshold, say — can induce mode competition that concentrates the field onto fewer modes and lowers \(r_{\text{eff}}\). Revision 1 stated this as generic collapse toward \(r_{\text{eff}}\to1\); that was too strong. In wave-chaotic and random resonators, semiclassical theory predicts that at sufficiently strong pumping the mean number of lasing peaks still grows with pump strength, as its 1/3 power.9 And disorder can work for dimensionality here: fragmenting transverse modes with random phase reduces their spatial overlap, which suppresses gain competition and lets more modes lase despite the lower Q.7

Mixing against readout noise. Revision 1 attributed the noise cost of strong mixing to the Petermann factor \(K\) diverging near exceptional points. The recent sensing literature points to a more general mechanism. Near a fixed-point bifurcation, responsivity and long-time noise are both amplified by the inverse of the critical relaxation rate, so the amplification cancels from the signal-to-noise ratio; non-orthogonality of the linearized modes (a dynamical Petermann factor) adds noise on top rather than setting the scaling.12 This is consistent with the absence of a fundamental SNR gain at linear exceptional points10 and with noise constraints found for nonlinear ones.11 These results concern frequency-shift sensing in self-oscillators; their application to computation is by analogy (§7, item 4). The working statement is that critical slowing down is the dominant shared amplifier of signal and noise near any instability, and mode non-orthogonality is a secondary contribution.

Gain against reproducibility. A substrate with gain must also return the same output for the same input history. In a numerical microcavity-laser reservoir, nonlinear memory capacity peaked near the onset of multimode lasing, at about 1.79 times threshold, and fell beyond it as spontaneous multimode oscillation made the output depend on the reservoir's initial state.8 The same study found that pumping extends memory beyond the passive \(1/\gamma_m\) by compensating loss. In that one numerical study, the regime that maximized nonlinear memory sat next to the regime that destroyed consistency.

These are tensions, not a demonstration that the requirements cannot all be met. The requirements are also of different kinds, and each is labeled: a necessary condition must hold for the substrate to compute at all; a threshold must clear a level set by the task; a figure of merit is continuous, where more is better. The open question is whether a single operating point satisfies all of the following at once:

Simultaneous requirements — does such a regime exist?
  • Nonlinearity in the recurrence — necessary conditionlowest nonvanishing kernel \(h_{k^{*}}\), \(k^{*}\ge2\), sourced inside the system boundary by route (a) or (b), input port declared, with \(\operatorname{supp}h_{k^{*}}\sim\tau_{\text{photon}}\) and attribution controls passed.
  • Memory matched to drive — necessary condition, threshold set by the taskspread\(\{\gamma_m\}\) (net of any gain) comparable to the symbol rate.
  • Consistency — necessary conditionthe same input history yields the same output; no self-oscillation or multimode instability that depends on initial state.
  • Readout above the noise floor — thresholdsignal above noise where mixing is strongest, with critical slowing near any instability counted against both signal and noise, and mode non-orthogonality as an additional penalty.
  • Dimensionality — figure of meritnoise-thresholded \(r_{\text{eff}}\) large relative to what the task needs, with \(\mathcal O(N_{\text{modes}})\) as the ceiling.
  • Modal overlap — diagnostic only (demoted in rev. 2.3)\(\delta=\bar\gamma/\Delta\omega\) is reported, not required (§3).

The linear-disorder half — the eigenfunction statistics and decay spectrum — is well-trodden open-wave-chaos physics. The unsettled half is the multimode nonlinear regime: whether a nonlinearity strong enough to register in a higher-order kernel on the photon-lifetime timescale can coexist with high modal participation and reproducible output. Whether the favorable region is nonempty remains the whole problem, and this revision does not settle it.

Status of claims (new in rev. 2.3)

Established: with linear media and data entering only through the drive, the internal state is a linear functional of the drive history (§2).

Plausible and testable: the requirements above constrain one another through one spectrum (§5); the kernel test and its attribution controls can separate cavity nonlinearity from instrument nonlinearity (§4).

Not established: that all the requirements can be met at one operating point; that they are a complete account of computation; and that the kernel test is practical at photon-lifetime timescales. These are the research program, not its conclusions.

§ 6

Survey of partially relevant systems

retitled in rev. 2.3

Each system below shows some of the requirements; none shows all of them together. This section is a survey, not validation of the framework. A pattern runs through the recent hardware results with nonlinearity built into the device: in each case the data enter through a pump or a parameter, not through the source term. That is the strongest practical reason for the port declaration in §4.

  • Integrated optical parametric oscillator reservoir13The closest match to (10) found so far. A thin-film lithium niobate OPO runs at about 10 GHz; cavity feedback serves as the optical memory, and the parametric nonlinearity sits inside that recurrent loop, coupling each pump pulse to the previous signal pulse. Data modulate the pump. The authors report that the OPO output had lower SNR than the pump.
  • Random network laser6Mode competition between overlapping lasing modes provides both excitatory and inhibitory nonlinearity; the input is the spatial pump pattern. Shows that many modes and strong nonlinearity can coexist in a disordered gain medium, but the computation is a single-shot steady state under femtosecond pumping, with no temporal memory.
  • Two-mode MEMS reservoir via blue-sideband excitation14A mechanical analogue: one drive at \(f_1+f_2\) activates two modes into their nonlinear regime, with the data amplitude-modulating the pump. Dimensionality doubles; the two modal quality factors give two memory timescales. Memory was tested only on NARMA-1.
  • Structural nonlinearity in multiple-scattering cavities1–3CW, low-power, tunable nonlinear order; kernel information capacity grows with structural nonlinearity, and parity up to fifth order was approximated.2 All static maps.
  • Structural nonlinearity in a reservoir4A second pass on a digital micromirror device gives a reservoir quadratic in the data, improving chaotic forecasting within about one Lyapunov time. Memory is a digital leaky integrator, not optical. A third-party automated review notes that the two compared arms also differ in diffuser, camera and loss; that critique is not yet checked (§7, item 3).
  • Ensemble averaging against device noise15Averaging over parallel physical systems to suppress noise. A possible way to pay for the high-noise, high-\(r_{\text{eff}}\) regime with hardware rather than by leaving it.
A practical caution for high-finesse cavities

Coherent speckle in cavity-based designs is sensitive to displacement, wavelength drift and thermal drift, which one group cites as the reason to avoid a cavity altogether.4 Another argues its globally paraxial Fabry–Pérot geometry is robust to absorption-induced thermal drift.3 The disagreement is unresolved, and it matters most for exactly the high-finesse disordered cavities this note considers.

§ 7

Open items

work in progress
  1. Structural nonlinearity with optical memory. The one lead that may combine a linear optical microcavity with temporal tasks5 has been located and its citation verified, but it has not yet been read. Until it is, the claim in §4 that no reviewed result shows a structural higher-order kernel on the photon-lifetime scale stands only for the sources listed here.
  2. Petermann statistics in chaotic open cavities. Whether the excess-noise factor in a disordered open cavity is better parameterized by modal overlap \(\delta\) than by proximity to exceptional points needs a source. No claim is made here until one is found.
  3. Confound in the structural reservoir comparison. Check whether the comparison in ref. 4 isolates the second modulator pass from the diffuser, camera and loss differences between its two arms.
  4. Transfer from sensing to computation. The noise argument in §5 rests on sensing results. A direct statement for reservoir readout near a bifurcation is still needed.
  5. Early-view citation. Ref. 2 has no volume or issue yet; recheck before any archival version.
  6. Comparison with other reservoir classes (new in rev. 2.1). State explicitly how the kernel test classifies extreme learning machines, delay-based reservoirs, and linear reservoirs with nonlinear readout. The last case is now addressed in §4; the other two are not yet written out.
  7. Proposed microwave scale-model test (demoted to an open item in rev. 2.2.1). Because the framework concerns waves in general, a tabletop chaotic microwave cavity with internal nonlinear elements could test the kernel protocol and the coexistence trade-offs cheaply. Chaotic microwave cavities have already been used for linear computation with a programmable metasurface.17 Such a model would test the framework, not any optical material. It stays a proposal until numeric pass and kill thresholds for each condition, and the parameter ranges to sweep, are registered.
  8. Citation detail (resolved in rev. 2.3.1). Ref. 17's volume and article number are now confirmed.
  9. Modal overlap (new in rev. 2.3). Before \(\delta\) returns as a requirement, give an argument linking resolved modes to computational capacity, or drop it.
  10. Evidence matrix (new in rev. 2.3). Tabulate each surveyed system against each requirement, using only what the full texts report. Deferred because several sources have been seen only as abstracts or excerpts.
