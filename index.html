A Coexistence Criterion for Computation in Driven Disordered Optical Cavities

Why high modal dimensionality is necessary but not sufficient, and the conditions a substrate must meet simultaneously.

STATUS — PRE-EXPERIMENTAL, WORK IN PROGRESS REVISION 2.1 — 2026-10-04 LICENSE — CC0 / PUBLIC DOMAIN FORM — SHORT NOTE
Work in progress. This note records a developing argument, not a finished result. Revision 2 narrowed the scope of the linearity constraint, added a sixth coexistence condition, and revised the noise condition in light of recent literature. Revision 2.1 generalizes the kernel criterion beyond second order and tightens one overstated sentence after external review. Sections changed are marked, and unresolved points are collected in §7. No experiment has been run.

A passive optical cavity filled with linear media, driven through its input field, implements a linear input–output map, however elaborate its engineered disorder. Such a map can be of arbitrarily high rank and still compute no nonlinear function of its input history. That statement holds only when the data enter as the drive. When the data instead modulate the cavity's scattering potential, multiple scattering produces a nonlinear map from data to output with purely linear optics.

The conditions under which a driven, open, disordered cavity becomes a genuine computational substrate are conditions on coupled physical observables — dimensionality, memory, a nonlinearity internal to the evolution, readout noise, and reproducibility — that all enter through the same complex spectrum. This note states those conditions and reduces the computation question to a measurable criterion on the Volterra kernels of the input–output map, with the input port declared.

§ 1

Setup: the open cavity as a non-Hermitian operator

Consider a resonator with a spatially structured permittivity \(\varepsilon(\mathbf r)\) — a plano-hemispherical cavity seeded with subwavelength dielectric scatterers. In the frequency domain the passive field obeys

(1) \[ \nabla\times\nabla\times\mathbf E(\mathbf r,\omega)-\frac{\omega^{2}}{c^{2}}\,\varepsilon(\mathbf r)\,\mathbf E(\mathbf r,\omega)=\mathbf 0. \]

Because the cavity is open — radiative leakage through the curved wall — the relevant operator is non-Hermitian, and its natural modes are quasinormal modes with complex eigenfrequencies

(2) \[ \tilde\omega_{m}=\omega_{m}-i\,\gamma_{m},\qquad \gamma_{m}>0. \]

The real part fixes the spectral position; the imaginary part fixes the linewidth and the photon lifetime. The scatterers break integrability and mix these modes.

§ 2

The linearity constraint and its scope

revised in rev. 2.1

For linear media, Eq. (1) is linear in \(\mathbf E\). The map from drive to field is therefore a linear operator \(A(\omega)\),

(3) \[ \mathbf E_{\text{out}}(\omega)=A(\omega)\,\mathbf E_{\text{in}}(\omega). \]

Equivalently, expanding the field in quasinormal modes, \(\mathbf E(\mathbf r,t)=\sum_m a_m(t)\,\boldsymbol\psi_m(\mathbf r)\), the modal amplitudes obey a coupled-mode equation

(4) \[ \frac{da_{m}}{dt}=(i\omega_{m}-\gamma_{m})\,a_{m}+\sum_{n}\kappa_{mn}\,a_{n}+N_{m}\!\left[\{a\}\right]+s_{m}(t), \]

where \(\kappa_{mn}\) is the disorder-induced linear mode coupling, \(s_m\) the projected drive, and \(N_m\) the coupling generated by any field-dependence of \(\varepsilon\). In the passive linear limit \(N_m=0\), and the solution is a convolution,

(5) \[ a_{m}(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\! G_{m}(t-t')\,s(t')\,dt'. \]

This is the constraint in one line: when the data enter only through the drive \(s_m(t)\), the internal state is a linear functional of the drive history. No matter how many modes participate or how complex \(A(\omega)\) is, the state never depends on products of its own past. A high-rank linear map can perform useful linear transformations — as a feature projection or a memory substrate — but it cannot realize nonlinear, state-dependent transformations of its input history.

Scope: the constraint is a statement about the input port

Eq. (5) assumes the data reach the cavity only through \(s_m(t)\). If the data instead modulate the scattering potential \(V\propto(\varepsilon-1)\) or the couplings \(\kappa_{mn}\) — a reconfigurable wall, a spatial light modulator acting as a mirror, an intracavity modulator — the field is still linear in \(\mathbf E_{\text{in}}\), but the map from data to output is not. Writing the response as a Born series in \(V\),

(5a) \[ \mathbf E_{\text{out}}=\left[V+V(G_0V)+V(G_0V)^2+\cdots\right]\mathbf E_{\text{in}}, \]

each additional interaction with a data-dependent \(V\) adds one polynomial order in the data.2 This structural nonlinearity has been demonstrated with CW light at low power in multiple-scattering cavities1,2 and in a Fabry–Pérot cavity with one mirror replaced by a spatial light modulator.3 The linearity constraint above therefore applies to source-port encoding only.

§ 3

Observables

revised in rev. 2

Dimensionality. Let \(M\) be the state matrix sampled by the readout, with singular values \(\sigma_i\). The effective dimensionality is the participation ratio of its singular spectrum,

(6) \[ r_{\text{eff}}=\frac{\left(\sum_i \sigma_i\right)^{2}}{\sum_i \sigma_i^{2}}, \]

set by the eigenfunction statistics (participation ratio, IPR) of the mixed mode field — not by the detector count.

Memory. The fading-memory timescale is the decay-rate spectrum measured against the drive period: \(\tau_m=1/\gamma_m\), with the cavity scale \(\tau\simeq \mathcal F L/\pi c\) for finesse \(\mathcal F\). Modal individuality is governed by the modal-overlap (Thouless) parameter

(7) \[ \delta=\frac{\bar\gamma}{\Delta\omega}, \]

with \(\Delta\omega\) the mean level spacing. Resolved modes require \(\delta\lesssim1\); for \(\delta\gg1\) resonances overlap and individuality is lost.

Nonlinearity. An activation internal to the evolution can enter by two routes.

(a) Field-dependent permittivity. The intensity route,

(8) \[ \varepsilon(\mathbf r)\;\to\;\varepsilon(\mathbf r)+\chi_{\text{eff}}\!\left(\mathbf r,|\mathbf E|^{2}\right), \]

for example saturable gain \(\chi_{\text{eff}}=\chi_0\,(1+I/I_{\text{sat}})^{-1}\) or a Kerr shift \(\Delta n=n_2 I\). What matters is not only its magnitude but its response time relative to \(\tau_m\): a nonlinearity slow compared with the photon lifetime acts as a quasi-static background, not a per-symbol activation.

(b) Data-dependent permittivity. The structural route,

(8a) \[ \varepsilon(\mathbf r)\;\to\;\varepsilon(\mathbf r)+\Delta\varepsilon\!\left(\mathbf r;u(t)\right), \]

needs no field buildup, so it is not limited by intensity. Its cost moves to the modulator, which must switch at a rate comparable to \(1/\tau_{\text{photon}}\) for the resulting nonlinearity to act per symbol rather than as a slowly reconfigured linear map. In this route the nonlinear order is set by the number of interactions with the modulated potential, which grows with photon lifetime: in a simulated two-dimensional scattering cavity, the coefficient of determination \(R^2\) of a linear fit falls steadily as wall reflectivity rises.2 The same spectrum \(\{\gamma_m\}\) that sets memory therefore also sets structural nonlinear order.

§ 4

The decisive criterion

revised in rev. 2.1

The computation question, free of any reservoir-computing vocabulary, is a statement about the Volterra expansion of the input–output map:

(9) \[ y(t)=\sum_{k\ge1}\;\int\!\cdots\!\int h_{k}(\tau_1,\dots,\tau_k)\prod_{i=1}^{k}u(t-\tau_i)\,d\tau_i. \]

A linear system has only a first-order kernel — \(h_1=G\) and \(h_{k\ge2}\equiv0\). Nonlinearity in the state evolution appears as a nonvanishing higher-order kernel, and the physically meaningful requirement is that it be sourced inside the cavity rather than by a nonlinear detector, with temporal support set by the photon lifetime. Let \(k^{*}\) be the lowest order above one whose kernel does not vanish:

(10) \[ \exists\,k\ge2:\;h_{k}\neq0;\qquad k^{*}=\min\{k\ge2: h_k\neq0\},\qquad \operatorname{supp} h_{k^{*}} \sim \tau_{\text{photon}}. \]

This collapses several fuzzy properties into one measurable quantity. In most cases \(k^{*}=2\): drive the system with impulses and impulse pairs and read off whether \(h_2\) survives over the memory window.

Why the criterion is not \(h_2\) alone (new in rev. 2.1)

Revision 2 stated the criterion as \(h_2\neq0\). That excludes genuinely nonlinear systems in which symmetry forbids the second-order term. If the map is odd in the input, so that \(u\to-u\) sends \(y\to-y\), every even-order kernel vanishes. A Kerr (\(\chi^{(3)}\)) cavity with coherent field encoding and phase-sensitive readout is such a case: \(h_2=0\) while \(h_3\neq0\). The criterion is therefore the lowest nonvanishing kernel of order two or higher. \(h_2\) remains the default probe because it is the cheapest to measure; when an odd symmetry is present, impulse triples are needed to resolve \(h_3\).

Why detector nonlinearity is not enough (new in rev. 2.1)

A linear scattering system read out by an intensity detector has only second-order nonlinearity, so it cannot approximate parity functions above order two.2 Detector nonlinearity therefore puts a hard ceiling on achievable order and adds no state-dependent recurrence. Nonlinearity inside the cavity, by either route, is not bounded this way. This is the main reason the criterion asks where the kernel is sourced.

The input port must be declared

Eq. (9) is incomplete until it states where \(u\) enters. With source-port encoding, a nonvanishing \(h_{k^{*}}\) certifies route (a). With parameter-port encoding, multiple scattering alone generates one, sourced inside the cavity with no \(\chi^{(3)}\). Either route satisfies (10) only if \(\operatorname{supp}h_{k^{*}}\sim\tau_{\text{photon}}\); for route (b) that requires modulation on the photon-lifetime timescale. Among the structural-nonlinearity demonstrations read for this revision, the maps are static or carry memory electronically: the one time-series forecasting result uses a digital leaky integrator for its reservoir state.4 None reviewed so far shows a structural higher-order kernel with support on the photon-lifetime scale (see §7, item 1).

Existing structural-nonlinearity metrics are complementary to the kernel criterion: the coefficient of determination of a linear fit and the mean number of interactions \(\langle n\rangle\),3 and the order-resolved Boolean coefficient sums \(S_n\) for binary inputs.2 None of them is time-resolved, which is what (10) adds.

A nonvanishing kernel of order \(k^{*}\) is a necessary criterion, not a sufficient one: it shows that nonlinearity exists, not that useful computation does. A substrate can be nonlinear and still compute poorly if its memory capacity or readout conditioning is weak; the sufficient figure of merit is task-level — memory capacity, or accuracy on a benchmark that demands nonlinear memory (NARMA, parity-\(N\)) measured against a linear-readout control. The standard task-independent measure is the information processing capacity, which decomposes a system's capacity by polynomial degree and delay; its degree-two part is, roughly, a task-level counterpart of \(h_2\).16 The same framework reports a general trade-off between the nonlinearity of computation and short-term memory,16 which is one face of the coexistence problem below.

§ 5

The coexistence problem

revised in rev. 2

By coexistence we mean the simultaneous satisfaction of the conditions below — not phase or modal coexistence, nor bistability. The difficulty is that the observables are not independent: disorder, loss, gain and the nonlinearity all enter through the same complex spectrum \(\{\omega_m-i\gamma_m\}\) and the same susceptibility.

Disorder against memory. Disorder raises \(r_{\text{eff}}\) but adds radiative loss, which shortens memory and drives \(\delta\) past unity. In the structural route the same loss also lowers the nonlinear order, since fewer round trips mean fewer interactions with the modulated potential.2

Nonlinearity against dimensionality. A nonlinearity strong enough to make \(h_2\neq0\) — gain saturation near threshold, say — can induce mode competition that concentrates the field onto fewer modes and lowers \(r_{\text{eff}}\). Revision 1 stated this as generic collapse toward \(r_{\text{eff}}\to1\); that was too strong. In wave-chaotic and random resonators, semiclassical theory predicts that at sufficiently strong pumping the mean number of lasing peaks still grows with pump strength, as its 1/3 power.9 And disorder can work for dimensionality here: fragmenting transverse modes with random phase reduces their spatial overlap, which suppresses gain competition and lets more modes lase despite the lower Q.7

Mixing against readout noise. Revision 1 attributed the noise cost of strong mixing to the Petermann factor \(K\) diverging near exceptional points. The recent sensing literature points to a more general mechanism. Near a fixed-point bifurcation, responsivity and long-time noise are both amplified by the inverse of the critical relaxation rate, so the amplification cancels from the signal-to-noise ratio; non-orthogonality of the linearized modes (a dynamical Petermann factor) adds noise on top rather than setting the scaling.12 This is consistent with the absence of a fundamental SNR gain at linear exceptional points10 and with noise constraints found for nonlinear ones.11 These results concern frequency-shift sensing in self-oscillators; their application to computation is by analogy (§7, item 4). The working statement is that critical slowing down is the dominant shared amplifier of signal and noise near any instability, and mode non-orthogonality is a secondary contribution.

Gain against reproducibility. A substrate with gain must also return the same output for the same input history. In a numerical microcavity-laser reservoir, nonlinear memory capacity peaked near the onset of multimode lasing, at about 1.79 times threshold, and fell beyond it as spontaneous multimode oscillation made the output depend on the reservoir's initial state.8 The same study found that pumping extends memory beyond the passive \(1/\gamma_m\) by compensating loss. The regime that maximizes participation is therefore close to the regime that destroys consistency.

The open question is whether a single operating point satisfies all of the following at once:

Simultaneous conditions — does such a regime exist?
  • Nonlinearity in the recurrencelowest nonvanishing kernel \(h_{k^{*}}\), \(k^{*}\ge2\), sourced inside the cavity by route (a) or (b), input port declared, with \(\operatorname{supp}h_{k^{*}}\sim\tau_{\text{photon}}\).
  • Dimensionality\(r_{\text{eff}}=\mathcal O(N_{\text{modes}})\).
  • Memory matched to drivespread\(\{\gamma_m\}\) (net of any gain) comparable to the symbol rate.
  • Modal individuality\(\delta=\bar\gamma/\Delta\omega=\mathcal O(1)\).
  • Readout above the noise floorper-mode signal above noise where mixing is strongest, with critical slowing near any instability counted against both signal and noise, and mode non-orthogonality as an additional penalty.
  • Consistency (new in rev. 2)the same input history yields the same output; no self-oscillation or multimode instability that depends on initial state.

The linear-disorder half — the eigenfunction statistics and decay spectrum — is well-trodden open-wave-chaos physics. The unsettled half is the multimode nonlinear regime: whether a nonlinearity strong enough to register in a higher-order kernel on the photon-lifetime timescale can coexist with high modal participation and reproducible output. Whether the favorable region is nonempty remains the whole problem, and this revision does not settle it.

§ 6

Evidence reviewed in revision 2

new in rev. 2

A pattern runs through the recent hardware results with nonlinearity built into the device: in each case the data enter through a pump or a parameter, not through the source term. That is the strongest practical reason for the port declaration in §4.

  • Integrated optical parametric oscillator reservoir13The closest match to (10) found so far. A thin-film lithium niobate OPO runs at about 10 GHz; cavity feedback serves as the optical memory, and the parametric nonlinearity sits inside that recurrent loop, coupling each pump pulse to the previous signal pulse. Data modulate the pump. The authors report that the OPO output had lower SNR than the pump.
  • Random network laser6Mode competition between overlapping lasing modes provides both excitatory and inhibitory nonlinearity; the input is the spatial pump pattern. Shows that many modes and strong nonlinearity can coexist in a disordered gain medium, but the computation is a single-shot steady state under femtosecond pumping, with no temporal memory.
  • Two-mode MEMS reservoir via blue-sideband excitation14A mechanical analogue: one drive at \(f_1+f_2\) activates two modes into their nonlinear regime, with the data amplitude-modulating the pump. Dimensionality doubles; the two modal quality factors give two memory timescales. Memory was tested only on NARMA-1.
  • Structural nonlinearity in multiple-scattering cavities1–3CW, low-power, tunable nonlinear order; kernel information capacity grows with structural nonlinearity, and parity up to fifth order was approximated.2 All static maps.
  • Structural nonlinearity in a reservoir4A second pass on a digital micromirror device gives a reservoir quadratic in the data, improving chaotic forecasting within about one Lyapunov time. Memory is a digital leaky integrator, not optical. A third-party automated review notes that the two compared arms also differ in diffuser, camera and loss; that critique is not yet checked (§7, item 3).
  • Ensemble averaging against device noise15Averaging over parallel physical systems to suppress noise. A possible way to pay for the high-noise, high-\(r_{\text{eff}}\) regime with hardware rather than by leaving it.
A practical caution for high-finesse cavities

Coherent speckle in cavity-based designs is sensitive to displacement, wavelength drift and thermal drift, which one group cites as the reason to avoid a cavity altogether.4 Another argues its globally paraxial Fabry–Pérot geometry is robust to absorption-induced thermal drift.3 The disagreement is unresolved, and it matters most for exactly the high-finesse disordered cavities this note considers.

Open items

work in progress
  1. Structural nonlinearity with optical memory. The one lead that may combine a linear optical microcavity with temporal tasks5 has been located and its citation verified, but it has not yet been read. Until it is, the claim in §4 that no reviewed result shows a structural higher-order kernel on the photon-lifetime scale stands only for the sources listed here.
  2. Petermann statistics in chaotic open cavities. Whether the excess-noise factor in a disordered open cavity is better parameterized by modal overlap \(\delta\) than by proximity to exceptional points needs a source. No claim is made here until one is found.
  3. Confound in the structural reservoir comparison. Check whether the comparison in ref. 4 isolates the second modulator pass from the diffuser, camera and loss differences between its two arms.
  4. Transfer from sensing to computation. The noise argument in §5 rests on sensing results. A direct statement for reservoir readout near a bifurcation is still needed.
  5. Early-view citation. Ref. 2 has no volume or issue yet; recheck before any archival version.
  6. Comparison with other reservoir classes (new in rev. 2.1). State explicitly how the criterion classifies extreme learning machines, delay-based reservoirs, and linear reservoirs with nonlinear readout. The detector ceiling in §4 settles the last case; the other two are not yet written out.
